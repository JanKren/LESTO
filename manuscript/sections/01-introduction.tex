Lead and lead--bismuth eutectic (LBE) are the coolants of the Generation~IV
lead-cooled fast reactor (LFR) and of accelerator-driven systems such as
MYRRHA \cite{Alemberti2014overview,Cinotti2011lead,AitAbderrahim2012myrrha,OECD2015handbook}.
A central argument in their safety case is that the liquid metal retains most
fission and activation products. The safety design criteria of the Generation~IV
International Forum quote volatilized fractions of the order of $10^{-6}$ for
\ce{^{137}Cs} and \ce{^{131}I} at \SI{700}{\celsius}. The same documents,
however, state that this retention still has to be verified for occupational
hazards and accidental source terms \cite{Alemberti2020lead,Alemberti2021safety}.
Equilibrium estimates for ALFRED indicate that iodine, cesium and noble gases
contribute significantly to the gas-phase source term once a fuel assembly has
failed \cite{Ivan2025assessment}.
Species that leave the coolant are carried by the cover gas and deposit on
colder structures, where they can cause localized contamination and later be
remobilized \cite{Neuhausen2020radionuclide,Aerts2020behavior}.
Where and how fast they deposit therefore links the coolant source term to the
activity that reaches the reactor hall.
Mechanistic source-term tools represent this link with lumped control volumes
or flow networks \cite{Hua2020development,Miyahara2023development}, and
retention and transport data are still missing for many elements
\cite{Trivedi2017retention}.
The Euratom project LESTO, which continues earlier work on the licensing of
ALFRED and MYRRHA \cite{Gianfelici2025research}, addresses the transport of
volatile fission products from the coolant to the reactor hall.

Most of what is known about these species in heavy liquid metals comes from
radiochemical experiments.
Henry's-law and transpiration studies supply the release data.
Iodine, for example, evaporates from LBE in significant amounts only above
about \SI{800}{K}, at a rate controlled by desorption at the interface rather
than by diffusion in the liquid
\cite{Neuhausen2006investigations,Ohno2006equilibrium,GonzalezPrieto2014equilibrium,Ivan2026evaporation}.
Speciation depends on concentration, atmosphere and wall material.
At trace level, iodine evaporated from LBE deposits as a single species
tentatively identified as \ce{BiI}
\cite{Karlsson2020thermochromatographic,Karlsson2021thermochromatographic}.
At iodine mole fractions of $10^{-5}$--$10^{-3}$ the deposits are \ce{PbI2} and
\ce{BiI3}, and \ce{BiI} disproportionates \cite{Liu2025chemical}.
An independent platform found \ce{PbI2}(g) to be the dominant species in dry
argon \cite{Liu2025exploring}.
These results were obtained with thermochromatography and
thermosublimatography. In both techniques a carrier gas transports the
volatile species along a column with a negative axial temperature gradient,
and the position of the deposit reflects its volatility
\cite{Merinis1969etude,Eichler1982evaluation,Gaggeler2014gasphase,Eichler1992volatilization}.
For weighable amounts, adsorption gives way to desublimation at about one
monolayer coverage \cite{Eichler1993complex}.
The onset of deposition is then commonly estimated from a saturation balance:
the evaporated mass, diluted in the total carrier volume, is set equal to the
equilibrium vapor pressure \cite{Liu2025chemical}.
In recent \ce{PbI2} experiments the measured deposition temperature fell with
both experiment duration and carrier flow rate \cite{Lobresco2026assessment}.
Earlier gradient-tube studies had found no flow-rate dependence
\cite{Helas1978investigation}.

Interpretation and modeling of such columns have remained one- or
zero-dimensional.
Monte Carlo models of the adsorption--desorption random walk assume an
idealized laminar flow \cite{Zvara1985simulation,Dietzel2024extended}.
Analytical kinetic models share this limitation \cite{Zhuikov2021kinetic}, and
the saturation estimate ignores transport altogether.
Nuclear reactor safety faces the same problem at larger scale.
The Ph\'ebus experiments showed that fission-product deposition concentrates
where wall and gas temperatures fall steeply, and that vapor chemistry, wall
condensation, homogeneous nucleation and thermophoresis act together there
\cite{Haste2013transport,Girault2013insights}.
The integral codes used to analyze these tests represent the circuit as a chain
of control volumes \cite{Cantrel2014astec}.
They underestimate condensation of supersaturated vapor in developing laminar
flow, disagree with measured retention where the flow is not fully developed,
and lack heterogeneous chemistry in thermal-gradient tubes
\cite{Kissane2006interpretation,Cousin2013modelling,Gouello2021interaction}.
Several studies have also shown that local gas-phase equilibrium is a poor
assumption for iodine at the short residence times of such circuits
\cite{Cantrel2003reaction,Xerri2012ab,Gregoire2017study}.
Other fields supply the physics these codes lack.
Boundary-layer theories of salt deposition and chemical vapor deposition
include thermal (Soret) diffusion of heavy vapors, variable properties, and fog
formation near cooled walls
\cite{Rosner1979chemically,Gokoglu1988significance,Holstein1988thermal,Castillo1989theory}.
Metal fog can enhance fission-product release from Pb and LBE pools by up to an
order of magnitude \cite{Epstein2020enhancement}, and \ce{PbI2} fluxes from
molten lead to cooled steel are consistent with such a fog model
\cite{BaniMelhem2026experimentalb}.
Resolved CFD of vapor and aerosol transport exists for sodium cover gases
\cite{Huang2019numerical,Ohira2022numerical,Anoop2026development} and for wall
condensation in containments, where face-flux boundary conditions were more
accurate and less grid-dependent than volumetric sinks
\cite{VijayaKumar2021implementation}.
None of this work, however, treats the laminar, strongly non-isothermal
transport of a heavy fission-product compound in a light carrier gas.

A predictive model must also represent chemistry. Speciation, phase stability
and vapor pressures of multicomponent systems are computed by Gibbs energy
minimization. The GEMS3K kernel was designed for exactly this purpose: it is
called in every control volume of a transport code whose composition,
temperature or pressure has changed \cite{Kulik2013gemselektor}.
Operator-split coupling of transport and equilibrium chemistry is mature in
reactive-transport modeling
\cite{Steefel2015reactive,Leal2017overview,Kosakowski2014opengeosysgem}.
Its splitting errors are well characterized, including a mass-balance error
under continuous boundary fluxes
\cite{Valocchi1992accuracy,Carrayrou2004operatorsplitting}.
In nuclear engineering, ANSYS Fluent has been coupled with the Gibbs-energy
solver of HSC Chemistry to model LBE coolant chemistry
\cite{Marino2020multiphysics}. OpenFOAM has been coupled with Thermochimica to
model fission-product retention and release in molten salts
\cite{Scuro2024coupleda,Scuro2024coupledb}. GEMS itself has been coupled with
the lumped severe-accident code MELCOR
\cite{Nichenko2021modelling,Nichenko2022msr}.
The main obstacle is cost: an equilibrium calculation can cost $10$--$10^{4}$
times a transport step \cite{Leal2020accelerating}.
Several approaches have been proposed to reduce it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item in situ adaptive tabulation \cite{Pope1997computationally};
  \item on-demand learning \cite{Leal2020accelerating};
  \item neural-network surrogates trained on speciation data
        \cite{Prasianakis2020neural,Prasianakis2025geochemistry}.
\end{itemize}
These couplings all concern liquid or aqueous systems, and the acceleration
strategies were developed for aqueous geochemistry or combustion kinetics.
None addresses the transport of a condensable vapor to a wall in a
high-temperature gas flow.

Lobresco et al.\ \cite{Lobresco2026assessment} presented the first CFD study of
a thermosublimatography column, using the T-Flows code and the \ce{PbI2}
experiments of the PSI setup \cite{Liu2025chemical}.
They compared two deposition models:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a temperature-activated relaxation model, calibrated against a
        reference experiment;
  \item a Hertz--Knudsen--Schrage (HKS) model driven by \ce{PbI2} vapor
        pressures from GEMS \cite{Persad2016expressions}.
\end{itemize}
Both models reproduced the decrease of the deposition temperature with
experiment duration. The vapor-pressure model, however, predicted systematically
broader downstream tails, which the authors attributed to unresolved
condensation mechanisms and represented by an empirical enhancement
coefficient.
They deliberately limited the scope to model assessment:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the carrier velocity was a prescribed, fully developed laminar
        profile;
  \item \ce{PbI2} was a dilute passive scalar in molar-concentration form;
  \item wall uptake was a volumetric source in a near-wall layer of fixed
        thickness;
  \item the results were obtained on a single mesh.
\end{itemize}
In the experiment, however, helium density changes by a factor of about 3.5
between the evaporation furnace and the cold end of the column. Because the
molar-mass ratio \ce{PbI2}/He is about 115, a mole fraction of only
\SI{1}{\percent} corresponds to a \ce{PbI2} mass fraction above 0.5.
Three effects were not quantified: thermal diffusion of the heavy species
\cite{Rosner2000heavy}, non-dilute transport \cite{Liao2007generalized}, and
nucleation in the cooling gas \cite{Barrett2000aerosol}.
The authors concluded that direct coupling with GEMS, and surrogates to make
it affordable, are the next steps. Liu et al.\ reached the same conclusion
from the experimental side \cite{Liu2025chemical}.
No CFD framework yet combines the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a variable-density carrier flow;
  \item gas--solid phase change that conserves mass exactly and does not
        depend on the mesh;
  \item equilibrium coupling for several condensing species;
  \item verification and validation against thermosublimatography data.
\end{itemize}

In this work, we develop and assess such a framework, implemented as the
OpenFOAM solver \texttt{rhoFixedFlowFoam} \cite{Weller1998tensorial}.
Our contributions are:
(i) a compressible, variable-density description of the helium carrier and a
mass-fraction formulation of species transport, with the temperature-based
and HKS deposition models written as wall fluxes that conserve gas plus
deposit exactly and are independent of the near-wall mesh;
(ii) a scaling analysis of wall uptake that separates transport-limited from
kinetics-limited deposition, and an assessment of what the empirical
enhancement coefficient represents%
